Practical guidance

Understand the behaviour. Decide what is safe for you.

Clear explanations, recognisable examples and practical steps. A single incident may have different explanations; repeated patterns, context and impact matter.

Manipulation & pressure

When someone makes you feel guilty for saying no

Pressure can be difficult to recognise when it comes from someone you care about.

“If you cared, you would…”
“I still have a choice.”

What might be happening?

Someone may be using guilt to influence your decision instead of respecting your choice. Disappointment is normal; repeatedly using guilt to control another person's choices is concerning.

What might you notice?

  • “After everything I have done for you…”
  • “You never do anything for me.”
  • They keep pressuring you after you have clearly refused.

What else could explain it?

A person may genuinely feel disappointed or may communicate poorly. Look at whether they accept your decision or repeatedly punish and pressure you for it.

What can you do?

  • Give yourself time before agreeing.
  • Remember that you are allowed to refuse.
  • You do not have to keep defending your decision endlessly.
  • If pressure becomes threatening, prioritise safety and seek support.

Words you can use

“I understand that you are disappointed, but my decision remains the same.”

Remember

Caring about someone does not mean surrendering your choices.

When should I seek help?

Seek support if pressure becomes persistent, affects your freedom, isolates you from others, involves threats, or makes you afraid to make ordinary decisions.

Bullying & harassment

When someone repeatedly humiliates or targets you

Bullying can happen at school, at work, in relationships, in public or online.

“It was only a joke.”
“I asked you to stop.”

What might be happening?

Repeated insults, humiliation, intimidation, exclusion or targeting can become bullying. A joke is not harmless simply because someone calls it a joke.

What might you notice?

  • Repeated comments about your appearance, identity or abilities.
  • Someone deliberately embarrassing you in front of others.
  • Threats, unwanted messages or attempts to isolate you.

What else could explain it?

One insensitive remark may be a mistake. Notice whether the behaviour continues after the person understands its impact, and whether there is a pattern of targeting or intimidation.

What can you do?

  • If safe, clearly state that the behaviour is unwelcome.
  • Keep a record of repeated incidents, dates and witnesses.
  • Consider a trusted teacher, manager, family member or appropriate support service.
  • Avoid confronting someone alone if you fear retaliation.

Words you can use

“Those comments are not acceptable to me. Please stop.”

Remember

You do not have to accept humiliation to keep the peace.

What if nobody believes me?

Try speaking with another trusted person or an appropriate reporting service. A clear written record may help explain what has happened. Your safety matters more than persuading someone who refuses to listen.

Boundaries & consent

When someone does not respect your boundaries

Your personal space, choices, privacy and consent deserve respect.

“I said no.”
“Your answer matters.”

What might be happening?

A boundary communicates what you are comfortable with and what you do not accept. Repeatedly ignoring a clear boundary is a warning sign.

What might you notice?

  • Someone continues touching you after you ask them to stop.
  • Someone reads your private messages without permission.
  • Someone keeps asking after you have refused.
  • Your personal space or time is repeatedly disregarded.

What else could explain it?

People may misunderstand an unspoken expectation. Communicating a boundary can clarify it, but you are not required to keep explaining a refusal to someone who is deliberately ignoring it.

What can you do?

  • State your boundary clearly if it is safe.
  • Move away from unwanted contact where possible.
  • Limit access to private information and personal accounts.
  • Seek trusted support if your boundary is repeatedly violated.

Words you can use

“I am not comfortable with this. Please respect my boundary.”

Remember

Consent must be freely given. Silence, fear or pressure should not be treated as agreement.

Do I need to explain why I said no?

You may choose to explain, but you do not need to prove that your boundary is reasonable before it deserves respect.

Control & autonomy

When someone tries to control your everyday life

Control may develop gradually and affect your freedom to make ordinary choices.

“You cannot meet them.”
“I have a right to relationships.”

What might be happening?

Controlling behaviour can involve restricting who you see, where you go, what you wear, how you spend money or how you communicate.

What might you notice?

  • Someone demands constant updates or access to your phone.
  • They discourage or prevent contact with friends and family.
  • They monitor your movements or finances.
  • You feel afraid of their reaction to ordinary decisions.

What else could explain it?

Concern, insecurity or disagreement alone does not prove coercive control. Repeated restrictions, monitoring, intimidation and loss of freedom are more concerning.

What can you do?

  • Notice whether your freedom and independence are shrinking.
  • Speak privately with someone you trust if it is safe.
  • Consider a safety plan before making major changes.
  • Do not announce plans to leave or confront someone if doing so could put you at risk.

Words you can use

“I value our relationship, but I also have the right to make my own decisions.”

Remember

Care and love should not require you to give up your freedom or live in fear.

What if I depend on this person?

You are not at fault for needing practical support. Consider confidential advice and gradual safety planning with a trusted person or specialist service. Do not take steps that could increase danger without considering your circumstances.

Threats & intimidation

When someone threatens or blackmails you

Threats should be taken seriously, even when the person says they are joking.

“Do what I say or else.”
“I deserve safety.”

What might be happening?

Threats use fear to pressure someone into doing something. Blackmail may involve threatening to reveal private information, images or messages unless demands are met.

What might you notice?

  • Threats of physical harm or retaliation.
  • Demands for money, access, images or contact.
  • Threats to expose private information.
  • Someone says they will harm you or another person if you refuse.

What can you do?

  • If danger is immediate, move to a safer place and contact local emergency services.
  • Do not meet the person alone or escalate the situation.
  • Preserve messages and other evidence if it is safe to do so.
  • Seek help from a trusted person and appropriate local authorities or specialist services.
  • If your device or account may be monitored, use a safer device to seek help.

Words you can use

You do not have to respond directly to a threat. Your priority is safety and support, not winning an argument.

Remember

Being threatened or blackmailed is not your fault. You deserve help without judgement.

Should I delete the messages?

If safe, preserve the original messages, account details, dates and screenshots. Do not put yourself at risk to collect evidence. A qualified local service can advise on safe reporting and evidence preservation.

Online protection

When someone pressures or harasses you online

Digital harassment can affect your privacy, confidence and sense of safety.

Repeated unwanted messages
Privacy. Safety. Support.

What might be happening?

Online harassment can include repeated unwanted contact, threats, impersonation, stalking, blackmail or sharing private material without consent.

What might you notice?

  • Someone creates new accounts to contact you after being blocked.
  • Threats to publish private images or conversations.
  • Attempts to obtain passwords or personal details.
  • Someone shares your information without permission.

What can you do?

  • Do not share passwords or verification codes.
  • Review account privacy and security settings from a safe device.
  • Save relevant evidence if safe, then report and block the account where appropriate.
  • Tell a trusted person. Seek specialist or official help for threats and image-based abuse.

Words you can use

“Do not contact me again.” Only send a message like this if doing so is safe and will not increase the risk.

Remember

You are not responsible for another person's decision to harass, threaten or exploit you.

What if private images are involved?

Do not blame yourself. Avoid forwarding or reposting the material. Preserve evidence safely and seek confidential support and appropriate local reporting options. If you are under 18, involve a safe, trusted adult or specialist child-protection service.

Silence & exclusion

When you feel ignored, excluded or unheard

Being left out can hurt. Understanding the context can help you decide what it means.

No reply yet…
There may be more than one reason.

What might be happening?

Someone may be unavailable, distracted, avoiding a difficult conversation or deliberately excluding you. Silence alone does not tell you which explanation is correct.

What should you notice?

  • Is it a one-time event or a repeated pattern?
  • Are you consistently excluded while others are included?
  • Does the person explain or discuss the issue when asked?
  • Is silence being used to punish, frighten or control you?

What else could explain it?

People may be busy, overwhelmed or need space. A delayed reply or a quiet expression does not prove rejection, anger or manipulation.

What can you do?

  • Allow reasonable time before assuming intent.
  • Ask calmly if something needs to be discussed.
  • Look at the broader pattern and how the relationship affects you.
  • Seek support if exclusion becomes persistent harassment or isolation.

Words you can use

“I have felt left out recently. Is there something we should talk about?”

Remember

Your feelings matter, even when the other person's intention is not yet clear.

Relationships & respect

When a relationship makes you feel uncomfortable or afraid

You can care about someone and still recognise behaviour that harms you.

“Maybe I am overreacting.”
“My feelings deserve attention.”

What might be happening?

Repeated disrespect, fear, pressure, humiliation, unwanted contact or control can make a relationship unsafe or unhealthy.

What might you notice?

  • You feel anxious about the person's reactions.
  • You change ordinary behaviour to avoid anger or punishment.
  • Your needs and boundaries are repeatedly dismissed.
  • You are pressured into decisions or intimacy you do not want.

What else could explain it?

All relationships can involve disagreement. The important questions are whether both people can express themselves safely, respect a refusal and take responsibility for their actions.

What can you do?

  • Take your discomfort seriously without forcing yourself to make an immediate decision.
  • Talk privately with someone you trust.
  • Consider professional support if it is accessible and safe.
  • If you fear harm, prioritise safety planning over confrontation.

Words you can use

“I want a relationship where we can disagree without fear or disrespect.”

Remember

Respect is not something you should have to earn by accepting mistreatment.

Supporting others

How to support someone who is being mistreated

Being heard without judgement can make a meaningful difference.

“You can talk to me.”
“I will listen.”

What can you do?

  • Listen patiently and let the person explain in their own words.
  • Take their concerns seriously without demanding proof immediately.
  • Ask what kind of support they want.
  • Respect their choices and avoid pressuring them to confront or leave.
  • Help them explore safe, appropriate support options.

What should you avoid?

  • “Why did you let this happen?”
  • “You should have known better.”
  • Making decisions on their behalf without considering their safety.
  • Sharing their story without permission, except where an immediate safeguarding duty applies.

Words you can use

“I am glad you told me. I am listening. What would help you feel safer right now?”

Remember

Support means respecting the person's dignity, choices and safety—not taking control away from them.

Your dignity matters

Know the rights and boundaries every person deserves.

These principles are a starting point for understanding respectful treatment. Specific legal protections differ by country and situation.

✋

Consent

People should be free to make voluntary choices without threats, force or pressure. Consent can be withdrawn.

🧭

Personal autonomy

Your choices, opinions and independence matter. Another person should not control your life through fear.

🔐

Privacy

Your personal information, communications and private space deserve appropriate respect and protection.

🌱

Dignity

You deserve to be treated as a person of equal worth, without humiliation, degradation or dehumanisation.

🗣️

Freedom to express yourself

You should be able to communicate concerns and disagree without intimidation or retaliation.

🛡️

Safety and protection

Everyone deserves protection from violence, harassment, exploitation and other forms of harm.

A practical way forward

Pause. Understand. Choose a safer response.

You do not need to solve everything at once. Use this simple framework to think clearly about a difficult interaction.

1

Observe

What exactly happened? Separate what you saw or heard from what you think it meant.

2

Understand

Consider context, impact and whether the behaviour is repeated. Avoid assuming intent from one gesture or phrase.

3

Verify

Where safe, clarify what happened. Notice whether the person listens, respects boundaries and changes behaviour.

4

Respond safely

Choose a response that protects your wellbeing. Seek support or distance when direct discussion is unsafe.

Your safety comes first

When you need protection or support.

If you are experiencing threats, violence, coercion or serious harassment, you do not have to handle it alone.

If you may be in immediate danger

  • Move to a safer place if you can.
  • Contact your local emergency service.
  • Reach someone you trust who can help you.
  • Avoid confronting the person if this could increase danger.

If the situation is ongoing

  • Consider a trusted friend, family member, teacher, workplace contact or qualified support professional.
  • Keep a record of incidents if safe to do so.
  • Consider a safety plan for communication, travel and important documents.
  • Seek local specialist, legal or protection services where appropriate.
Important: If someone monitors your phone, browsing or accounts, using this website may leave a trace. Consider a safer device or private support channel if you are concerned. Do not change settings or delete history if doing so could alert someone and increase your risk.
Humanity begins with listening

Helping someone can begin with believing they deserve respect.

You do not need to have every answer to support another person. Listening without judgement, respecting their choices and helping them find safe options can matter greatly.

✓
Listen without blame

Give them time to explain what they are experiencing.

✓
Respect their decisions

Do not pressure them to take actions they are not ready or safe to take.

✓
Help them find support

Explore trusted people and qualified services together.
